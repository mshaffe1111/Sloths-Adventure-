# How real studios make levels, and what our AI team should copy

Caveat on method: this session's network blocked direct fetches of nintendo.com, iwataasks.nintendo.com, gamedeveloper.com, wikipedia.org, cognition.ai, yachtclubgames.com and tinysubversions.com, so for those pages I worked from search-engine excerpts of the primary page, not a full read. Anthropic's engineering posts were read in full.

## 1. Roles and who owns a level

**Nintendo EPD (Mario).** A director owns the whole game's feel; a producer (Tezuka on Wonder) sets direction and the big asks. Ideas come from everyone, not only level designers: on Wonder, "everyone from programmers to designers and sound designers joined in and wrote down gameplay ideas on sticky notes", over 2,000 of them, and "we made prototypes on the spot" (https://www.nintendo.com/ph/interview/aqmx/03.html, https://www.techradar.com/gaming/nintendo-switch/super-mario-bros-wonder-devs-had-over-2000-ideas-for-the-game-including-the-addition-of-an-enormous-live-action-mario-that-didnt-make-the-cut). Tezuka then set a per-course rule: "how about creating a Wonder for each and every main course?", and director Mouri wanted "at least one element in each course that would surprise or delight players" (https://www.nintendo.com/ph/interview/aqmx/03.html). On 3D World, only "about 1 out of 50 ideas" made it in, with about 1,000 more in reserve (https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/wiiu/super-mario-3d-world/0/3/). Hayashida says the key is settling a core concept per level, which he enforced rigorously from 3D Land on (https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-secret-to-i-mario-i-level-design). Individual course designers still bring personal challenges ("I set some sort of challenge for myself, like being able to clear a course without ever touching the ground", NSMB Wii) (https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/wii/nsmb/1/4/). NSMB Wii had about 20 people on levels (https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/wii/nsmb/2/3). Exact designer counts for 3D World and Wonder: unsourced.

**Ubisoft Montpellier (Rayman Origins/Legends).** A level design director (Olivier Palmieri) plus a written method, "rational design", taught in an internal Design Academy: mechanics are broken into "atomic" parameters so difficulty is controllable, and levels must "explore the mechanics well, rather than filling the game with one-shot gameplay mechanics" (https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/rational-design-the-core-of-i-rayman-origins-i-). Origins was made by "five to ten people" (https://www.gamesradar.com/rayman-origins-beyond-good-evil-and-state-games-industry-interview-michel-ancel/); Legends grew to about 90 (https://www.everyeye.it/articoli/provato-rayman-legends-20036.html).

**Valve (Half-Life).** The "Cabal": groups of 4-5, half level designers, half programmers, owning one area of the game; bigger groups "diluted" meetings, smaller ones lacked ideas. Half-Life 2 moved to three nearly independent design teams, each owning about a third of the game, plus a shared art team (https://developer.valvesoftware.com/wiki/Cabal_process, https://gdcvault.com/play/1013450/Valve-s-Design-Process-for).

**Small teams.** Celeste: Maddy Thorson led level design; the GDC talk is the primary source (https://gdcvault.com/play/1024307/Level-Design-Workshop-Designing-Celeste). Hollow Knight: two co-directors split design and art, and start from a place ("a cavern with a lake") and a progression plan rather than an empty room (https://www.gamesradar.com/games/action/team-cherry-explains-the-silksong-development-process-that-was-so-fun-devs-just-couldnt-stop-if-youre-trying-to-build-a-mario-level-you-might-take-an-empty-space-and-think-oh-should-there-be-a-few-platforms-here). Shovel Knight: every level starts from a theme, and new mechanics "sprung up from the needs of each individual level"; one Specter of Torment level took about four intense months (https://www.yachtclubgames.com/blog/specter-of-torment-level-design-deep-dive-1-5).

**Spelunky (generated).** One designer hand-authored room templates per role (drop-in, landing, corridor, off-path), typically 8-16 per type, placed on a 4x4 grid along a guaranteed solution path, with "probabilistic tiles" so each copy plays differently but never impossibly (https://www.gamesradar.com/spelunky-retrospective, https://www.tinysubversions.com/spelunkyGen2/index.html). This is the closest real model to our generator: variety comes from many authored chunks plus rules, not from rerolling.

## 2. The pipeline per level

The common shape: concept on paper, greybox (blockout), playtest and iterate, approval, art pass, polish. Moon Studios (Ori) states it plainly: a strong concept on paper, a blockout of platform polygons, repeat "until everyone likes the blockout", which earns a "design approved" stamp, then hand-off to artists (https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/q-a-designing-the-gorgeous-metroidvania-i-ori-and-the-will-of-the-wisps-i-). A published studio workflow lists the gates in order: design document sign-off, greybox test and approval, asset list approval, beautification review, final validation (https://hilarious-surfboard-69f.notion.site/Level-Design-Workflow-1b4b3d8419254d359872892634e69012). Practitioners stress that greybox changes are cheap and settling the layout before art avoids rework (https://ratloopgamescanada.com/dev-blog/greyboxing). NSMB Wii sketched levels on paper before data entry (https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/wii/nsmb/1/4/).

**Cuts.** Cutting is normal and large: 1 in 50 ideas on 3D World (above); Kondo's live-action Mario was cut from Wonder (https://www.nintendo.com/ph/interview/aqmx/03.html); Team Cherry cut a whole zone (Boneforest) and called it "totally the right thing to do" (https://pcgamer.com/hollow-knight-devs-say-axing-boneforest-zone-was-totally-the-right-thing-to-do). Silksong's first area was added late because playtests showed the start was too harsh (https://www.gamesradar.com/games/action/silksongs-first-area-was-only-added-after-team-cherry-realized-it-needed-a-softer-intro-were-trying-to-be-kind-of-respectful-to-the-player-were-not-trying-to-baby-them). Who approves: the director (Mario, Ori "everyone likes it"); in Valve's model the cabal itself plus playtests.

## 3. Variety, and isolation versus shared review

Tools studios use: one core concept per level (Hayashida, above); a per-course quota (one Wonder per main course, one surprise per course); a written method so each mechanic is explored fully before moving on (Rayman); and a director who curates from a huge idea pool (2,000 notes, 1-in-50 survival).

**Isolation or shared review?** The evidence splits by phase:

- **Idea generation alone works better.** Decades of research show pooled individuals ("nominal groups") produce more, and often better, ideas than people brainstorming together, mainly because of "production blocking" (https://www.uni-muenster.de/imperia/md/content/psyifp/aeechterhoff/wintersemester2011-12/seminarthemenfelderdersozialpsychologie/08_diehl_stoebe_productivityloss-brainstorming_jpsp1987.pdf). Interacting groups also cover fewer idea categories (https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1046496413486701). Nintendo's sticky notes are individual writing, pooled later.
- **Building and review are shared, never blind.** NSMB Wii "gathered everyone together and held a meeting to watch each other play", so the team came to share one standard where before it had been all over the place (https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/wii/nsmb/2/3). Galaxy's director deliberately sought "as many of the play testers' opinions as possible" (https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/wii/super_mario_galaxy/1/2). Valve's teams were "nearly independent" by area but under shared playtests and art (above). I found no studio that builds levels in deliberate isolation from one another and ships the result unreviewed (unsourced as a negative, but nothing turned up).

## 4. Rules and documents

Metrics and method docs: Rayman's atomic parameters (above); Mario's four-step "introduce, develop, twist, conclude" structure, analysed from 3D World (https://www.engadget.com/2015/03/17/super-mario-3d-world-design/). Level design documents signed off before greybox (Notion workflow above). Milestones: vertical slice is a small section at near-final quality that sets the bar for art, audio, UI and performance; alpha means playable start to finish but rough; beta means features frozen, content final, only bugs left (https://pctechmag.com/2026/06/unity-production-milestones-what-each-stage-should-deliver/, https://www.wayline.io/blog/setting-game-development-milestones-concept-launch). A formal per-level "definition of done" from a named studio: unsourced.

## 5. Teams of AI agents (2025-2026)

- **Lead plus parallel helpers works for parallel, read-heavy jobs.** Each helper needs "an objective, an output format, guidance on the tools and sources to use, and clear task boundaries"; vague briefs caused duplicate work and gaps. Multi-agent runs cost about 15x the tokens of a chat. Coding and work where "all agents must share the same context" are poor fits, and agents "are not yet great at coordinating and delegating to other agents in real time" (https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system).
- **Split-up builders drift apart.** Cognition's example: split "build Flappy Bird" into a background helper and a bird helper and you get a bird and background "with completely different visual styles"; their rule is to share full context and traces (https://cognition.ai/blog/dont-build-multi-agents).
- **Long jobs need written state, not memory.** A progress file, git history and a full feature list (all starting as failing) let fresh sessions pick up; agents tended to "mark a feature as complete without proper testing" until told to test end to end in a real browser like a user; editing the tests to pass is forbidden (https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-harnesses-for-long-running-agents).
- **Failure study.** 1,600+ traces across 7 frameworks show 14 failure modes in three groups: bad specs, agents misaligned with each other, and weak verification/termination; gains over single agents were often small (https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.13657v2).
- **Role persistence vs fresh agents:** the sources favour fresh agents with clean context plus written handoffs over long-lived ones (Anthropic, both posts). A head-to-head study: unsourced.

## What our AI team should copy

1. **One core concept per stage, written as a one-page pitch before building.** The lead approves the pitch (Hayashida's core concept, Ori's paper concept).
2. **A big idea pool, harshly curated.** Ask many helpers for mechanic ideas separately, pool them, keep the best few (Wonder's 2,000 notes, 3D World's 1 in 50). Cuts are expected, not failure.
3. **Greybox gate before art.** No Hugging Face spend until the blockout plays well on a phone and the lead stamps "design approved" (Ori, studio gate list). This also protects the $72 budget.
4. **A shared rules doc with numbers.** STAGE-RULES.md is our Rayman "atomic" sheet; every brief carries it, and every builder's first appearance of a mechanic is taught and then explored, not used once.
5. **Shared watch-each-other-play review.** At each check-in, the lead (and a reviewer helper) plays or watches every stage side by side, so the set reads like one game (NSMB Wii meeting).
6. **Written state and end-to-end proof.** Each helper keeps a progress file and a checklist that starts failing; "done" needs phone-size screenshots or video of a real playthrough, never a self-report (Anthropic harness).
7. **Tight briefs with boundaries.** Objective, output format, which files, what not to touch (Anthropic research system), and give builders the full shared context (art bible, palette, existing pieces), not just their slice (Cognition).
8. **Spelunky-style chunks for generated worlds.** Hand-authored section templates per role with controlled randomness, 8-16 per role, is the proven way to get variety.

## Where real studios disagree with the 8-blind-builders idea, and where they support it

**Support.** Separate idea generation is backed by research: people working alone and pooled later produce more and more varied ideas than groups (Diehl and Stroebe). Studios split ownership: Valve's nearly independent teams by area, Nintendo designers each with their own course challenge. So eight builders, each owning a stage or world and dreaming up its gimmick without seeing the others first, has real support.

**Disagree.** No studio found ships work made blind. Every case adds shared review: watch-each-other-play meetings (NSMB Wii), many playtesters (Galaxy), a director curating and cutting (Wonder, 3D World), shared art direction across separate teams (Valve HL2). Without it you get the Flappy Bird problem: parts that each look fine but clash in style and difficulty, plus duplicate gimmicks no one spotted. The AI research adds that multi-agent setups fail most often on specs, inter-agent misalignment and verification, and cost far more tokens.

**Suggested middle path:** blind for the pitch, shared for everything after. Eight builders each write a one-page pitch alone; the lead pools them, removes duplicates and picks; then builders work in their own stage but against one shared rules doc and art bible, with shared review at every gate, and the lead (Matt's director) has the final cut.
