Sloths story: the road to A+

Oct 29, 2026 · @Matt Shaffer

The short answer

The story is a B today against the best story games, and it can be an A+ against indie mobile and an A against story games without changing the plot. The bones are already Pixar-grade: everyone has lost someone, nobody has anyone to blame, slow is set against late, and a grieving grandfather has turned lateness into a crime. What holds it back is how the story is wired, how it sounds, and how little of it you get to play.

This is the final story report. It pulls together part one and part two of the story review in the polish report (SR-1 to SR-43 keep their numbers here), adds what was still missing, and turns it all into one list of edits for the coders. Everything was checked against the script on the test-link branch on Oct 29. The story files there match main, so the line numbers hold. No code was changed.

  ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Bar                                                                              Today   After the words (steps 1 to 3)   After everything (steps 1 to 10)
  -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- ------- -------------------------------- ----------------------------------
  Indie mobile story games (Florence, Old Man's Journey, Alto's Odyssey)           A−      A+                               A+

  Story games of this size (Celeste, Ori, Undertale, Hollow Knight, Paper Mario)   B       A−                               A

  Pixar at its best (Up, Toy Story 3, Inside Out, Coco)                            B−      B+                               A−
  ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

An honest note on the top bar. A Pixar film is performed by actors and animated frame by frame, so words alone stop at about B+ against it. The last steps there are delivery: sharp pictures, timing, a beat of silence before the big lines, and voices. With those, A− is realistic. A full A+ against Pixar would take a studio, and it isn't the bar that decides whether people love this game.

The eight moves that get it there, biggest first:

1.  Let the three relationships finish face to face. Pokey and Meeca never speak in the finale. Pokey answers Dot's confession to the General, not to her. Flicker never meets Dot. Those three missing exchanges are the main reason the big moments land small.

2.  Fill the holes in the story. The biggest: nobody ever says why the General took Meeca, or why he came that morning. Pokey's parents and who raised him are blanks too. So is a moment where being slow costs something, which is what makes "slow is not stopped" earned instead of just said.

3.  Take out the AI sound. It's mostly rhythm and formula, not word choice. Dot is meant to be the breathless run-on talker, but in the script her sentences are as short as Pokey's. All ten boss scenes follow the same five beats, and too many characters talk in wise one-liners.

4.  Sharpen each character. Dot gets more mothering in the middle worlds. Meeca gets her people-pleasing shown, not just told. The General gets his tough-guy shell (decided). Every guardian gets one beat that breaks the boss template.

5.  Play it like an RPG. Dot comments as you play, the way a party member does. Pokey keeps a journal in his own voice. Side characters come back in later worlds. The garden fills up as you free people. And the game notices what you did.

6.  Hide the rest of the story. Every hidden thing today tells the General's or Grandma's story. Add finds that tell Pokey's, Dot's and the guardians' stories, each one paying off later.

7.  Make the peak something you do. The hand Pokey holds out in the thunder should be the player letting go with one thumb, not a picture.

8.  Deliver it. A table read, a kid test, sharp story art, and short sound barks for each voice.

What's missing

Nine holes, in order of how much they cost the story. The first three are the ones a careful adult notices on a first play. Each comes with drafted lines in the character's voice, and three of them are Matt's call (marked).

1.  Why the General took Meeca, and why that morning. Nobody ever says. The clues are all there: the flower opened for Meeca for the first time since Grandma, the narrator says the thorns sleep while it's open, and the soldiers arrived the same morning. But a player has to work out alone that her flower was undoing his Pact, and nobody ever names the personal reason, which is fear. An RPG villain without a stated motive reads as a plot device. Fix it in three places, each in its own layer:

    -   The public reason, at tea (storyMemories.ts, after 103). Queen: "Her flower puts our thorns to sleep, darling. The morning it opened, his whole Pact started to yawn." Kids get a sleepy thorn; adults get the politics.

    -   The excuse, in the tower (storyMemories.ts, after Meeca's question at 260). Meeca: "Why am I here, Pell?" Pell: "For your safety, Miss. He says so every morning. I think he says it to the mirror first."

    -   The truth, in the finale (storyFinale.ts 89, replacing it). General: "She waited for help, and it came late. So I put you somewhere nobody would ever be late for you." Then "I should have knocked" (90), as it is. Now that line answers something.

2.  Pokey and Meeca's reunion. The whole game is a slow friend coming for her, and they don't say one line to each other in the finale. The drafted scene is in Big moments below.

3.  Being slow never costs anything. Every time Pokey is slow, the story proves him right. That makes "slow is not stopped" a slogan instead of something earned. The best stories let the hero's virtue cost him once. The cheapest place is the gap closing in World 6, which today just happens ("They found the gap and closed it"). Make it the tea (storyWorlds.ts 264, Matt's call):

    -   Dot: "That was the last note I can get out. They sewed the gap shut the night before I got there. One night, Pokey."

    -   Pokey: "We stopped for tea."

    -   Dot: "We stopped for tea."

    -   Then the scene goes on as it is. Now his stop in the sealed-note scene is a real low point: he's sitting with the cost. It also gives the General a line at boss 10: "You stopped for tea, boy. The General counted." Pokey: "Yes, sir. It was very good tea." The answer is a joke, but he means it, which is the whole theme.

4.  Pokey's answer to Dot, to her face. Drafted in Big moments.

5.  Flicker. He guides Meeca in 5-M, says "I was the light" to Pell, and then disappears from the story. Matt's Oct 27 plan (Flicker joins Pokey after Dot leaves, Dot comes back jealous, they make peace before the mountain) is drafted word for word in reference/STORY-REVIEW-2026-10-27.md and was never built. Build it, with the mother's jealousy line in Relationships below.

6.  Pokey's parents (Matt's call). We know they went out on a stormy night and didn't come back, and that Pokey remembers his father's claws on the bark and his mother humming. We never learn what they went out for. My recommendation is the plainest answer: they were out gathering, like every evening, and the storm turned early. Nothing heroic, nobody's fault. That's exactly what Grandma's letter says about her own death ("It was only weather"), so the theme holds for both families. Show them once, without words: one of Matt's large painted backgrounds behind the singing-storm memory (storyMemories.ts 135 to 136), two sloths on the far branches with the sky turning. Give them one find too (the growth-notch twig, under Collectibles).

7.  Who raised Pokey. He was a cub when his parents died, and the game never says who took him in. The scenes suggest Grandma's garden half-raised him: he's there watering the fern as a cub, and he knows her door. Make it one line in the garden memory (storyMemories.ts, after 83). Grandma: "You're staying for supper, Pokey. You always are." That makes Grandma his loss too, so when he forgives the lateness that killed her, it's his own grief he's letting go of.

8.  The argument the General can't win. The Cast Bible names it: Pokey is the one person with a real reason to blame lateness, and he doesn't. The game never stages it. One exchange at boss 10 does (storyBosses.ts, after 181). Pokey: "I've had a long time to work out who to blame, sir. It was a storm." General: "The General does not lose twice." The shell slams shut because he has no answer.

9.  The forest, and everyone in it, after. We're told the forest is fading (cloudy water, unkind rivers) but never see it fade or heal. And only the Queen, the Golem and Pell get an ending; the other seven guardians, the squirrels, Gruntlewick and the mailman mostly don't. RPGs end with a card for everyone you met. That's covered under Playing it like an RPG.

Two things I'd leave unsaid. Meeca's parents: a third set of lost parents would make the forest feel like a sad place, and the grandparents are the point. Make sure no line contradicts that. And the Pact's magic stays as simple as it is: thorns, collars, a horn, and a song that puts them to sleep. Kids don't need more rules, and adults enjoy filling it in.

Getting rid of the AI sound

The AI sound isn't in the words. It's in six patterns that repeat until a reader can feel the machinery under the scene. I measured them across the five story files: about 11,000 words in 694 lines. Any one of them alone is fine, and good writers use all of them. It's the stacking that readers notice.

1. Everybody talks in the same short rhythm. This is the biggest one. The Cast Bible says Dot is "long, breathless and escalating" and Pokey is "quick, flat and short". In the script they sound the same:

  -----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Voice         Typical sentence   Sentences of 6 words or fewer   Should be
  ------------- ------------------ ------------------------------- ------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Pokey         4 words            83% (90% in boss scenes)        Right. Short and dry is him.

  The General   2 to 3 words       97%                             Right. Clipped is him.

  Dot           4 words            68%                             Wrong. She's the run-on.

  Meeca         4 words            70%                             Too short. She's a warm host who asks questions and talks over people.

  Narrator      9 words            29%                             Fine, but even. Let one sentence per scene run long and loose.
  -----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Dot's long speeches are built from short sentences stuck together ("I know what I said. I know what I said."). Someone who rambles doesn't use full stops. She uses "and" and commas, and she interrupts herself. The fix: in every scene she's in, give Dot one real run-on sentence. When she goes short, it should mean something is wrong, which is exactly how the Cast Bible says her silence works.

2. One template for ten bosses. Every boss scene runs the same five beats: a taunt about how slow he is, Pokey's dry comeback, the fight, wounded pride with a second comeback, then the guardian softens and points the way north. Fifteen lines in the boss file have a laugh sting under them, nearly all of them Pokey's comebacks, and there are 54 across the story. That's a laugh track. Five guardians end by giving directions, like quest-givers, when the petal on Pokey's wrist already points north. Fix: break the template in at least four bosses (each is listed in Character by character), cut four of the five direction lines, and keep one laugh sting per scene, on the line that needs the help.

3. Too many wise sayings. The game has nine or ten lines that could go on a poster: "Slow isn't stopped", "It's only weather", "Peace is mostly paperwork", "Be patient with the slow ones. They always arrive", "Fast people are the ones who arrive in time", "That's all I ever do. Then the one after", "You're the one who stops", and the secret token's "Be slow with him. He has been fast for far too long." When everyone is wise, it sounds like one writer being wise through all of them. Fix: Grandma owns the wisdom, and hers always comes attached to a fern, a kettle or a bell. The General's sayings stay, because they're the wrong belief the story argues with. Everyone else says the plain or the wrong version.

4. Every line is a setup or a punchline. Exchanges are clean: a question, a perfect answer, a button. Real people talk past each other, guess wrong, and say things that go nowhere. Fix: in each scene, allow one line that isn't a joke and isn't plot, just someone being a person. The script already has a few, and they're among its most human moments: "Is this branch taken?", "I checked it with my face", "Thanks for not interrupting".

5. The narrator tidies up. "Didn't say anything" or "said nothing" seven times, "for a long time" six times, 33 narrator sentences starting with "Then", "nobody" 23 times, "both hands" eight times. Each closes a scene with a little bow. Fix (SR-1 and SR-3): at most one narrator button in every three scenes, "for a long time" twice in the whole game, "nobody" under ten, and "both hands" only where the finale needs it.

6. Every note has the same shape. "Pokey," then a funny or brave paragraph, then "Love, M.", and five of the ten have a P.S. joke. Real letters change shape with the writer's mood, and Meeca's state is the whole point of her notes. Fix: let the shape show how she's doing. One note crossed out and started again. One cut off mid-sentence when the guard comes. One that's only a drawing. The sealed note's handwriting, falling off the end of every line, is already the model.

Rules for anyone writing a new line, the Worlds 7 to 10 pass included:

-   Read it out loud. If a good actor would refuse to say it, rewrite it.

-   The swap test: if another character could say it and nothing changes, it isn't finished.

-   Never describe what the picture already shows.

-   Only the General counts, and Meeca's tally. Nobody else's jokes use numbers.

-   One wise line per act, and it's Grandma's.

-   End two scenes out of three on dialogue, not the narrator.

-   Dot gets one real run-on per scene. When she's short, something's wrong.

-   Every scene has one laugh a six-year-old gets without reading, and one line that's just a person being a person.

-   Banned: "both hands" (except the finale), "something enormous", "for a long time", "Nobody saw", and a guardian giving directions.

Character by character

Matt's definitions come first, then what's on screen, then what an A+ version needs and the exact edits. A character is A+ when you'd know their line with the name covered, and when they want something, hide something and change by the end.

Pokey: a smart-aleck, but good-hearted

On screen he's on model from the first line to the last: quick, flat jokes aimed at himself, polite words for bad news, and kindness that shows in what he notices (four bowls of the wrong soup). Two things keep him from A+. He's sometimes the straight man while Dot gets the jokes, and his flaw never costs him anything. His real flaw isn't slowness, it's that he jokes his way around hard conversations. So his arc should end with him having the two conversations he's been avoiding, one with Dot and one with Meeca.

-   Give him the comeback where Dot fusses. After storyWorlds.ts 55 (Dot: "Don't move. Don't you move."), Pokey: "Historically, not a problem."

-   Let slowness cost once: the tea closes the gap (What's missing, 3).

-   Give him the argument the General can't win at boss 10 (What's missing, 8).

-   Night-Wing Monarch, storyBosses.ts 147: "I hope I said something nice." (SR-31)

-   Magma Drake, storyBosses.ts 168: "You sound terrible. Have some water. Actually, don't." (SR-32)

-   His two straight lines are in Big moments: "You can sit down now" to Meeca, and "And you came back every time. I was counting" to Dot. Neither has a joke in it. That's the payoff the Cast Bible promises: "by the end he says one true thing with no joke attached."

Meeca: has to get people to like her, does what people want

She's well built in her notes and in the best scene in the game, "No. You don't get to say that yet." What's thin is that we hear about her people-pleasing in her angry speech more than we see it cost her. She also has a quiet arc nobody made on purpose: she thanks Pell for a blanket she didn't ask for (World 5), refuses the pillow (World 8), and says "No" to her grandfather (finale). Build that on purpose.

-   Make sure she never says a plain "no" to anyone before World 8.

-   Add the soup note, funny and sad at once, as the World 6 note's first paragraph (finds.ts, w6_note) or a new note: "Pokey, I told Pell the soup was lovely. It was not lovely. Now he brings it every day, and every day I say it's lovely. I don't know how to stop." Kids laugh at the soup; adults see the trap. Her finale line "I ate all of it. I said thank you every time" then lands as a payoff.

-   Let her notes change shape with her mood (AI sound, pattern 6). World 7's note starts with "I'm fine" crossed out, then "I'm tired." World 9's breaks off mid-sentence when someone who isn't Pell comes up the stairs.

-   In her own stage, give her flavor and keep the tips short (SR-18): "Too high. A chair! Somebody thinks of everything." and "A rope. Test it first, Grandma says. Testing."

-   The reunion and the bush line with Dot are in Big moments and Relationships.

Dot: motherly, almost Pokey's second mother

She reads motherly in bursts (the prologue, World 1, the ledge in World 4), then mostly anxious and comic through the middle, gone for World 7, and her ten-world payoff is Pokey saying "Okay." Her confession in World 10 is excellent. The fix has four parts.

-   Her rhythm. One real run-on per scene (AI sound, pattern 1). Rewrite storyWorlds.ts 60 as shown there; 255: "That's worse, that's so much worse, I'm fine, I've backed up about nine feet, I'm going to stay over here and be fine from a distance!"; 336: "I'm not humming. I'm vibrating. Humming is on purpose." (SR-21); 367: "Pokey, you've been quiet since the lava, which is long even for you, and you're doing the thing with your eyebrow." (SR-23)

-   Mothering acts, not just words, spread through the middle. World 3, before the dark (after storyWorlds.ts 143): "Have you got everything? Petal? Satchel? Ears?" World 4, at the Sky Sovereign (storyBosses.ts, after 82): "He's very polite, he gets that from me, I mean he doesn't, I'm a bird, but he gets it from me." At tea (storyMemories.ts, after 96): "Sip. Don't gulp. She's watching you gulp." World 8, back from the net (storyWorlds.ts 316, before her news): "Never mind me, look at you, you've lost weight, have you been eating, you haven't, I can tell from here." That last one calls back her very first worry in the prologue ("Have you been eating?"), so the mother comes back when she does.

-   A mother's jealousy, not a rival's, with Flicker (Relationships).

-   Her payoff. The finale exchange (Big moments), and if the player found all of her lost pocket, The Notch (Collectibles).

One fix outside the game: the Cast Bible calls Dot "he" throughout. She's "she" in the game and in VOICE.md, and the Bible should match. Add one line to her entry too: it started as penance and became love.

The General: a tough guy who cracks and turns back into who he was (decided)

The full plan is in part two of the polish report: in public he calls himself "the General", never "I"; the cracks show in private (the spoons, the door) and once in public, at boss 10 ("I find that I mind"); and the turn back is the man in the photo books, who retires and plants trees by the river. About a dozen lines, starting with "The General does not knock." This report adds three:

-   His motive, in the finale, replacing storyFinale.ts 89: "She waited for help, and it came late. So I put you somewhere nobody would ever be late for you."

-   The cost of the tea, at boss 10 (after storyBosses.ts 179): "You stopped for tea, boy. The General counted." Pokey: "Yes, sir. It was very good tea."

-   "The General does not lose twice" moves to right after Pokey's new line about the storm, so the shell slams shut because he has no answer.

Flicker: short, dry, earnest, holds his ground, and guilty

He's a D today because, in Pokey's story, he doesn't exist. Build Matt's Oct 27 plan as written. Then give him what every other character has, a physical tell kids can watch for: his light stutters when he isn't telling the whole truth. It pays off twice. When he first meets Pokey and says he's "just passing", his light stutters. And at the gate, when Dot says "Flicker, stay with him" and he says "I will", it holds perfectly steady. Add his entry to the Cast Bible (he/him, short and dry, earnest, guilty about the one tree his light led Meeca to).

Pell, Grandma, and the people around them

-   Pell is the best supporting character in the game. Protect him. His one addition is the "For your safety, Miss" line (What's missing, 1).

-   Grandma is perfect, because every line is attached to a thing. Add "You're staying for supper, Pokey. You always are." (What's missing, 7) and fix the secret token so it sounds like her (AI sound).

-   The two soldiers who never spotted Dot ("Is that a hummingbird?" "Where?") are a gift. Bring them back twice: at the open gates, still not seeing her, and in the weeks after as Pell's first customers.

-   The squirrels ("North! At that speed? See you next year!") never see him come home. In the weeks after, give them one line: "Back already?"

-   Gruntlewick and the mailman already pay off beautifully. Leave them alone.

The reunion (new, after storyFinale 101)

Right now Pokey climbs a thousand steps for Meeca and the game never lets them say hello. The scene with her grandfather is so good that nobody notices on the first play. They notice on the second. Put this straight after "Mostly."

  Narrator: Meeca turned around. Pokey was standing where he'd stopped, soaked, in the middle of the courtyard.

  Meeca: You came.

  Pokey: I was in the neighborhood. Slowly.

  Narrator: She laughed. Then her back stopped being straight.

  Pokey: You can sit down now.

  Narrator: They sat on the wet steps. Neither of them said much. It was the best conversation of the year.

The last line is the caption from the World 4 photo book. Players who found it get the callback. Players who didn't still get a good line. "Her back stopped being straight" pays off the earlier "Her back was very straight" at storyFinale 82. She held herself up for 191 nights. This is where she stops.

Pokey answers Dot (replaces storyFinale 126 to 128)

Dot's whole arc is guilt turning into love, and at the moment she confesses, Pokey says "Okay." It reads like he's hurt and closing the door. That's the wrong ending for the spine of the game.

  Pokey: I thought you came around because you liked me.

  Dot: I did! I do! It was both. I came because I owed you, and I stayed because... I stayed. You're mine, Pokey. You've been mine since you were this big and couldn't reach the low branch.

  Pokey: You were late every time, you know.

  Dot: I know.

  Pokey: And you came back every time. I was counting.

  Narrator: Dot's wing went to the pouch at her side, which was empty, and she left it there.

"I was counting" turns the General's habit inside out. He counted the hours help was late. Pokey counted the times it came anyway. Nobody needs to point that out.

Letting go, played (storyFinale 46 to 52)

The whole game teaches one thing about Pokey: he holds on when it thunders. Make the player hold on too, then let the player be the one who lets go.

1.  The thunder scene starts with a prompt to hold both thumbs down. Pokey grips the wall. Every roll of thunder shakes the screen a little.

2.  A second prompt asks the player to lift one thumb. Pokey's hand comes off the wall and reaches toward the General.

3.  Thunder breaks. If the player puts the thumb back down, Pokey grabs the wall again, and the scene waits. No fail, no timer, no text. It just waits for the player to try again.

4.  When the player keeps one thumb up through the thunder, the song starts from the window.

First-time players like Sally will get it on the second try, and that's the point. On Easy, the prompt stays on screen. On Normal, it fades after the first try. This is the one place in the game where the controls are the story, and it's what turns a good ending into the moment people tell their friends about.

The keepsakes: three on screen, the rest in the satchel

If a player finds all ten keepsakes, the finale stops for up to ten little scenes in a row. Each is lovely. Together they bury the ending. Keep three as full scenes, in this order, because each one does something the others don't:

1.  The ribbon (World 4). The two cut halves tied with string. It's the General finishing his grief.

2.  The key and the music box (World 1). It plays the song, which closes the loop from the tower.

3.  The bell (World 10). "Somebody is coming home." This is the last keepsake shown, always.

The locket, medal, petal, rattle, bear, stopwatch and seeds don't disappear. Each becomes one line of dialogue on its own page in the satchel (see Playing it like an RPG), opened from the end screen or the hub. The stopwatch exchange ("I let you.") is too good to lose, so if the player found it, it plays as a short tag after the end credits. The seeds stay where they are, on the hill.

1.  The tower. Thunder, the played let-go, the song, the collars fall, the General turns.

2.  Meeca and the General, with the new motive line at 89.

3.  The reunion. Pokey and Meeca on the steps.

Playing it like an RPG

The way to think about this: Sloths plays like a platformer and should remember like an RPG. The jumping stays fast and fun. What changes is that the world notices what you did, your companion talks to you while you play, and the things you pick up mean something. Paper Mario, Ori and Hollow Knight all do this inside action games. None of it needs dice, stats or menus full of numbers.

Here are ten pieces, roughly in the order I'd build them. The first three do most of the work.

1. Dot as a party member who talks

Right now Dot speaks in cutscenes and gives tips in play. In an RPG, the companion is who you spend the game with. Give her about 80 short in-stage lines that trigger at rest points: a checkpoint, standing still for five seconds, the top of a long climb, the moment after a hard section. Ten or so per world, two to four seconds each, never during a jump.

They're not tips. They're her talking. A few examples in her voice:

-   After a long climb: "You did that whole wall at one speed, do you know how annoying that is to watch, it's very impressive, it's very annoying."

-   Standing still in World 3: "Are you resting or are you thinking? With you I can never tell. Both? It's both."

-   After a near miss: "I didn't see that. I'm choosing not to have seen that."

-   World 8, after she's back: "I'm not going anywhere. I'm just saying it so it's said."

The rule from SR-38 holds: 12 words or fewer for a story aside, and tips stay separate. Her lines change after she leaves and comes back, so World 8 to 10 Dot sounds different from World 1 to 6 Dot. That change is her arc, and the player feels it without a cutscene.

2. Pokey's journal

Every stage cleared adds one line to a journal in Pokey's voice, written as if he's slow and careful with a pencil. It's the save screen and the chapter select in one. Examples:

-   1-1: "Left home. Dot says I forgot something. I didn't. I checked twice."

-   2-5: "A snake asked me to be nice to it. I was. It was fine."

-   6-8: "Dot left. I'm going to keep writing in here like she didn't."

-   9-10: "A thousand steps tomorrow. I'm going to take them one at a time. There isn't another way."

This is where the quiet Pokey gets an inner voice. It's also where a player who stopped for a month remembers what was going on.

3. The satchel as an inventory

The narrator says "he put it in the satchel" sixteen times. Make the satchel a screen instead. Everything Pokey picks up goes in it with a picture and one line: Meeca's notes, the keepsakes, the photo books, Dot's lost things, guardian relics. Tapping a keepsake plays its one line of dialogue. This is also where the seven keepsakes that leave the finale live, so nothing a player found is ever lost.

4. People who come back

RPG worlds feel real because you meet the same people twice. Sloths already has the cast for this:

-   Gruntlewick the crier, World 2. His flood warning gets one sentence further every time you pass him. On replay it's further along. He finishes it in the finale.

-   The two soldiers from the prologue. You see them again at the gates in World 9, more tired each time. In the weeks after, they're Pell's first customers.

-   The squirrels. Every time Pokey walks back through World 1 on the map, one of them says "Back already?"

-   Pell. His soup shows up in Meeca's notes, his face at the tally, and his crooked shop sign at the end.

-   Flicker. He shows up once in World 5 and once in World 7 before his story pays off.

5. The Heartbloom garden as a hub

Meeca's garden is where the story starts and ends. Make it the place you go between worlds, even if it's only a single screen. Each guardian you free sends something there: the Prowler's ledge stone, a strand of the Queen's silk, a feather from the Sky Sovereign, the Drake's two cups. Rescued critters from World 2's reed cages and World 4's eaglets move in. By World 10 the garden is full, and the one empty spot is the chair by the flower. It fills after the finale.

This gives the player something they built, and it makes "the whole family" on the hill something they can see.

6. The world notices what you did

Small, cheap, and it's what makes people say a game remembers them:

-   The General counts. At the gates and in Boss 10, he quotes the real number of times the player fell and the real number of in-game nights. "Forty-one falls, boy. The General counted."

-   If the player found Meeca's World 6 note, Dot mentions the soup in World 8. If not, she doesn't.

-   If the player beat Thornbark, the Golem's ending card mentions him.

-   If the player found all of Dot's lost things (see Collectibles), her finale line gets one extra sentence.

None of these are branches. The story is the same. It just notices.

7. Coats that mean something

Coats unlock by points now. Make four of them story items:

-   The leaf raincoat, from Grandma's garden memory. Wearing it in a thunder stage gives Dot a line.

-   Meeca's scarf, unlocked after the finale. It drags on the ground in every stage.

-   The General's rust scarf, unlocked by beating the game on Normal.

-   Pell's coat, tailored, unlocked by finding his shop sign. Slightly crooked.

The rest stay as they are. Four that mean something make the other forty feel like a wardrobe instead of a list.

8. The map warms up

The world map starts gray under the Pact. Each freed guardian brings color back to their world. By the end the whole map is green, and the iron thorns are gone. It's a progress bar that tells the story.

9. Ending cards for everyone

After the finale, one card per character: a single painted picture and one line. Examples:

-   Venom Fang: "Pokey stops by on Thursdays. He always asks first."

-   The Prowler: "He still sits on the ledge. Now somebody sits with him."

-   The Sky Sovereign: "She lets the wind lane run where it likes."

-   The Night-Wing Monarch: "Pokey told her how the dream ended. She wrote it down."

-   The Magma Drake: "Two cups. Both used."

-   The two soldiers: "Pell took their measurements. They didn't need new coats. They just wanted to sit down."

-   Flicker: "He stays at Pell's shop. The light over the counter is steady."

These replace the weeks-after narration that's trying to cover everyone in a paragraph.

10. A playable prologue morning

Right now the prologue is a cutscene. Let the player walk Pokey through one ordinary morning in the garden before the General arrives: water the fern, nudge the snail, find Meeca, get told he looks thin. Two minutes, no danger. It teaches the controls, and it means the player has a home to lose before it's taken. Every great RPG opening does this.

What not to add

No stats, no experience points, no shops, no dialogue choices. Sloths is a story about a sloth who does one thing at one speed. Choices would fight that. Let the player change how much of the story they see, never what happens in it.

Collectibles and hidden things

The rule for every find: it should tell the player something they couldn't learn any other way, and it should pay off later. A coin is a reward. A letter is a story. Sloths already has the bones of a great collection. It needs fewer kinds of thing, each carrying more story, and every one of them paying off somewhere.

Under the Ruins: Pokey's note

The secret level has Pokey pushing a note up to Meeca, but the words were never written. Here they are:

  Meeca. I'm coming. It's taking a while. I'm fine. P.

  P.S. My handwriting's wobbling. That's the rain.

It's the one note in the game going the other way, and it uses her own "I'm fine" back at her. At tea in the finale, if the player found it:

  Meeca: Your handwriting wobbled.

  Pokey: It was raining.

  Meeca: You were underground, Pokey.

New: Dot's lost pocket

One find per world from World 4 to World 9, six in all. Each is something small that fell out of Dot's pouch over the years: a button, a bent pin, a feather that isn't hers, a scrap of the yellowed envelope, a tiny map with Pokey's tree circled, a ribbon that matches nothing. Each has one line from Dot, in the run-on voice, when Pokey picks it up.

Together they tell her side of the story before she tells it herself. The last one, the envelope scrap, is a corner of the letter she's been carrying for years. Players who found all six figure out the confession a world early, which is exactly what a good hidden track should do. Finding all six adds one sentence to her finale line.

New: The Notch

One hidden find in the memory scenes: a twig with growth notches cut into it, one a year, in two different hands. The early notches are his parents'. The later ones are Grandma's. It answers who raised Pokey without anybody saying it, and it sits in the satchel next to the painted background of his parents.

New: guardian relics

Each of the ten guardians, Thornbark included, leaves one thing behind after their fight, hidden somewhere in the next world rather than handed over. The Prowler's ledge stone. A strand of the Queen's silk. The Monarch's notebook page with Pokey's sleep-talk on it. The Drake's second cup. Each relic goes to the garden hub.

Find all ten and the ruined council chamber in World 7, the one with the Golem's chairs, lights up. A secret scene plays: all ten guardians sitting in the chairs, as they were before the Pact, with the Golem finally saying his whole sentence to someone. This is the platinum reward that means something.

New: a snail in every world

The snail from the prologue is on the same trip Pokey is. Hide him once in every world, a little further along each time, always moving left to right, never in a hurry. Tapping him gives nothing but a tiny sound. Players who find all ten get one extra line at the very end, from Pokey: "I saw him every day, you know. He never once stopped for tea."

Small hidden things that cost almost nothing

-   The squirrels' sign in World 1. On replay it says "BACK ALREADY?"

-   The soldiers' trail. Their dropped helmets and a half-eaten ration show up in Worlds 3, 6 and 9, so their return at the gates makes sense.

-   Meeca's window. In every world, a lit window somewhere in the far background, getting closer each time. In World 10 it's the tower.

-   Gruntlewick's warning, one sentence further every time you pass.

-   Pell's shop sign, hidden in World 10, crooked. Unlocks his coat.

How many is too many

About five story finds per world: note, keepsake, book, and up to two of Dot's pocket, a relic, or the snail. More than that and finding things becomes a chore, and the platformer slows down. Sally should be able to finish a world without feeling she missed the story. Jen and Steph should be able to go back for the rest.

Steps 1 to 3 together are the A+ against indie games. Step 4 is what gets it to an A next to the big story games. Steps 5 to 10 are what makes people call it an RPG.

One caution for the coders: story.test.ts checks the script. After step 1, run it, and update any test that was checking for a line that got cut. A failing story test after a text edit almost always means the test was pinned to old words, not that something broke.

How to test that it landed

Three tests, each with a different job.

A table read with Matt, Jen and Steph. Read the whole script out loud, each person taking a voice. It takes about an hour. Anywhere someone stumbles, or two characters sound the same, mark it. This is the best test there is for AI sound, because nobody can say an AI line out loud without hearing it.

A kid test. One player around 8 to 12, playing on Easy, with no help. Afterward, ask them three things: who was their favorite, what was the saddest part, and what happened to Dot. If they can answer the last one, the story is clear. If the saddest part they name is the tea or the reunion, the new scenes are working.

Sally as the story test. She's the most valuable tester for this, because she plays for the story. Watch for one thing: at the end of a world, does she ask what happens next? If she does in Worlds 6, 8 and 9, the stakes are landing. Also watch whether she gets the let-go on her own. If she needs to be told twice, make the prompt clearer. If she gets it and goes quiet, it's done.

A good sign across all three: people quote lines back afterward. If someone says "It was very good tea, sir" a day later, that's the A+.
